# Third playtest pass, 2026-09-05 — the table becomes a table

Thirteen items from one session at the client (docs/debug-ui-design.md is
the table's design; this is the pass that changed it). Most are
presentation, two were real bugs, and one of the bugs was the worst shape a
bug can have.

---

## 1. The seats are a grid, not a line

`seatColumns(n) = clamp(ceil(n / 2), 1, 3)` — half the seats in each of two
rows, capped at three across so a mat never gets too narrow to read. Six
seats give the owner's "3 on top, 3 on the bottom"; four give 2 and 2. It
is one CSS custom property (`--seat-cols`) set from the seat count, because
the number of seats is a fact the renderer already has and the stylesheet
does not.

**Seating ORDER is untouched.** Your prey is on your left, and that is a
rule of the game (p. 15), not a layout preference — so the mats wrap in
seating order rather than being rearranged to put "you" anywhere in
particular.

## 2. A face in each seat

`SeatFace { avatar, bot }`, keyed by seat id, passed to the table by the
shell. A player's picture comes from their own profile; a **bot gets a
glyph, never a borrowed face**, because the entire point of a thumbnail is
telling people apart and a bot that looked like a player would defeat it. A
player with no picture gets their initial.

A remote player's avatar travels on the `join` message and rides in
`LobbySeat.avatar` — **re-checked by `avatarProblem` on arrival**, because
that one came over the wire from somebody else's client and the profile's
own check happened on their machine, not this one.

None of it is game state: the engine knows a seat by its id and nothing
else, so no avatar reaches the command log and a save replays the same game
without one.

## 3. The hand is always YOURS

`LocalTransport.setLocalSeat(seat)`. `view()` masks to that seat always,
instead of following whoever is being asked.

Following the deciding seat is a **hotseat** rule — it exists because
several people share one screen — and applying it to a solo game took the
player's own hand away from them every time a bot was thinking, which is
precisely when there is time to read it. Null (hotseat) keeps the old
behaviour, so nothing existing moved. It is the same masking rule and the
same subject `stateFor` already uses for a peer.

The hand is still only PLAYABLE on your own decision: on somebody else's it
is drawn to be read, marked `hand watching`, and lights nothing.

## 4. The play strip

The combat strip has always shown a fight in progress, large enough to
read; every other card flashed past in the log and was gone. `actionStrip`
is the same idea widened to the whole game: every `cardPlay` frame on the
stack (there can be several — a cancel is played inside another card's
as-played window), plus the action they are being played into.

Beside it, the numbers that decide a block: the acting minion's **stealth**
and every possible blocker's **intercept**, with a blocker whose intercept
has caught up marked. **This leaks nothing** — the acting minion is face
up and both totals are sums of face-up cards, which is the same argument
`docs/ai-v1-design.md` §3 makes for putting them in `PlayerView`. It saves
a player adding the table up by eye.

## 5. The ash heap was reading a number that meant nothing

`render()` computed `CardBurned` events ÷ seats. **Measured over a whole
game: `CardBurned` fires ZERO times.** Burning is one of four ways into the
heap and, in practice, the one that never happens — a discard, a card in
play burning, and an action card resolving are the others. Meanwhile
`SeatState.ashHeap` — the real zone, built 2026-09-01 — was sitting there
unread.

It is now that field, and the pile is a **button that opens for ANY seat**,
because p. 16 makes the ash heap the one fully public zone in the game
("can be examined by any Methuselah at any time"). Cards, not names.

The test pins the zero directly, so the reason the old number was wrong is
recorded rather than described.

## 6. Looking through a pile shows cards

A search's candidates are card IDs in a zone the viewer cannot read —
`redactFor` masks every library **including its owner's**, since you may
not read your own deck (p. 14). So the only place a candidate's name
existed was inside the option's label, as prose.

**`LegalOption.answerChoice.card` carries the name**, backfilled centrally
in `choiceOptionsFor` rather than by each handler that builds a card
choice: there are a dozen of those and a thirteenth would forget. That is
the `backfillCentralQueries` pattern, and the richer-options principle —
the engine is the only thing that knows what an option is about, and when
it does not say, every consumer re-derives it.

Nothing is revealed that the answer did not already reveal: this is the
option list the searching seat is being offered, and the option list IS the
search. A test asserts the library is still face down in the masked view,
and that "Find nothing" — an ordinary answer, not a decline (p. 48) —
carries no card name and stays a plain button.

## 7. The preview gets out of the way

The magnifier is a full-size card that follows the pointer, so while
dragging one it covered the very targets being dragged at. It hides on
**`mousedown`**, not `dragstart` — it is in the way from the press, not
from the moment the browser decides a drag has begun.

## 8. A card that greys out stops being selected

`isSelected = playable && selected === c.id`. A card clicked open stayed
lit and lifted after the decision moved on and greyed it out, so the table
showed a highlighted card with nothing behind it.

## 9. Card text has a size

`settings.cardTextPx` (default **15**, up from a hard-coded 11), offered as
Small / Normal / Large / Very large, clamped on load for the same reason
the AI pause is. The scan is glanced at; the text is read word by word, and
the two do not want the same size.

## 10. Leaving

A Leave button, confirmed, and only shown when there is somewhere to go
back to (`canLeave` — the playtest snapshot has no menu behind it). It
closes the room and the connection on the way out: a host who walks away
without doing that leaves a room code on the broker that dials into
nothing.

## 11. Auto-pass already worked — the control was not findable

The report was "auto-pass should work even if the player is not a bot".
**It does**, and `tests/ui/autopass-human.test.ts` pins it at a table with
one human and three bots, **with a control case** asserting the same table
without the toggle IS shown those passes — or the test would pass for the
wrong reason.

What was wrong was discoverability: the toggle is per seat and off by
default (the owner's standing rule that nobody is ever skipped), and the
list showed four bare names with nothing saying which was yours. Your own
seat is now marked "(you)" and the note says in as many words that it works
for your seat too.

## 12. THE BUG: an ally that pays its last life mid-action

Reported as *"the action buttons disappear and I can't do anything"*, which
is the worst shape a bug can have — the game is not over, and there is
nothing on screen to answer.

An ally's life is held in the same field a vampire's blood is (p. 11), so
"burn 1 blood to play this" spends a point of life, and an ally at 1 life
pays with the last of itself. `burnDepleted` then removes it **in the
middle of its own action**. `currentBleed` read the actor with `getMinion`,
which throws — out of `settle`, out of `decision()`, out of `paint()`,
leaving the previous markup on screen with its buttons already disabled.

**Every other reader of `af.acting` in the engine already used
`findMinion`** for exactly this reason — "a minion can leave play at any
point, so read it with `findMinion`, not `getMinion`" is a lesson the
combat sweeps learned and wrote down. `derived.ts` no longer imports
`getMinion` at all, with a comment saying why: **a derived read must be
total**, because a throw from there surfaces as a game that cannot be
answered rather than as an error anyone can act on.

A bleed with nobody doing the bleeding is worth **0**, and the event-log
fold is skipped with it — those modifiers are bonuses TO a bleed, and there
is no longer a bleed to add them to.

The two halves are not the same problem, which is why the test has both: a
**reaction** is played by a bystander, so the frames outlive it (that path
was always fine); a **modifier** is played by the acting minion, and that
is the one that broke.

**And the UI now says so.** `paint()` catches, prints the error and the
advice that `logs/` replays everything up to it, then rethrows. An engine
error must never again be indistinguishable from a frozen table.

## 13. A guest names themselves

`setName` on the lobby protocol. The name still ARRIVES from the joiner's
profile — that is the right default — but changing it in the lobby is a
separate act. **The host decides the final name**, because a seat name IS
the engine's seat id and two seats cannot share one, so a clash is numbered
exactly as an arrival's is; the guest learns the result from the next
broadcast rather than assuming its request stood. Refused once the game has
started: by then the name is threaded through the command log, the event
log and the save file.

The box repaints on **blur**, not per keystroke — the same rule the
new-game seat rows follow, for the same reason.

A test pins that renaming keeps the deck, and keeps the SAME deck: the
fingerprint is taken over the deck's contents and does not depend on the
seat, so asserting the hash is unchanged is stronger than asserting a deck
is still there.

## 14. Two fixture traps, both familiar

A walker taking `options[i % n]` never got round to playing a card, so the
ash-heap test found an empty heap and reported "the fixture is wrong"
rather than a bug. **Agents play a real game; index-walkers play the
board** — the same lesson the crypt waves learned seven times.

And the avatar test's second guest was **turned away as "table full"**, so
their avatar was null for the wrong reason — the negative-test trap this
project keeps finding, now caught by asserting the guest was seated at all
before asserting anything about them.

## 15. Actions live on the cards they are about (owner request)

The hand became the interface on 2026-08-29: a card with a legal play is
lit, badged with how many plays it has, and clicked. Everything a MINION
or a CARD IN PLAY could do stayed in a list of buttons at the bottom of
the screen — named by ids the player had to match against the table by
eye. Now it does not.

### Which card an option is about needs no id parsing

`LegalOption` already carries it structurally: `minion` on `takeAction`,
`declareBlock`, `burnForIntercept`, `burnForUnlock`, `cancelBlock` and the
three influence options; `source` on `useAbility` and `useEntryAction`;
`castVote.source`. That is the richer-options principle paying out a
second time — the engine is the only thing that knows, and when it does
say, a consumer does not have to re-derive it. Parsing option ids for this
would have been a second, drifting model of the option vocabulary.

### An option lights EVERY table card it names

A granted rush appears both on the card granting it and on the minion that
would perform it; a diablerie appears on the diablerist and on their
victim in somebody else's torpor region. The badge means "things you can
do involving this card", which is true of each of them, and both are
places a player looks.

### THE SAFETY PROPERTY, and it is the whole of the risk

An option indexed under a key nothing renders would vanish from the table
AND from the bar, and **a missing option looks exactly like an illegal
one** — the failure shape this project has found in card filters a dozen
times, now one layer up in the UI.

So `actionsByTableCard` takes the STATE and indexes only under ids the
table actually draws (`tableEntityIds`). `castVote.source` is the case
that proves it necessary: it is a minion id, or `card:<id>`, **or "edge"
or "caller"** — and those last two are not cards. They are dropped from
the index and stay on the bar, where they belong.

Two tests pin it from both ends: every key is on the table, and every
option is reachable from exactly one of the table, the hand, or the bar.

### What stays on the bar

Measured across the windows: `turn.minion` keeps only **End minion
phase** (nine buttons became two badged minions plus one); `action.effects`
keeps **Pass**; `turn.influence` keeps **Pass** and the **crypt draw**,
which is about no card on the table because the card is not there yet.
Strikes, presses, referendum terms and choice answers likewise have no
card to sit on.

Both empty groups on the bar are signposts — "click a lit card on the
table ↑" beside the hand's existing one — so a player who has read the bar
all game is told where the buttons went.

### Details worth keeping

- **Click only; a table card is not draggable.** Dragging a hand card ONTO
  these tiles already means "play this card here", and one gesture cannot
  mean two things on one element.
- **The inner tile wins.** Attached cards nest inside their minion, so a
  click that lands on an attachment is about the attachment.
- **The menu grows DOWN.** A hand card at the bottom of the screen has no
  room below it; a table card does.
- **Nothing is lit while an AI's move is paced.** That decision is the
  computer's, and the table must not offer to answer it any more than the
  bar does.
- **A click opens the menu, always — one option or five.** Both halves of
  the screen behave the same way, and see §16.

## 16. A click is "show me", never "do it" (owner request)

A card with exactly one legal play used to resolve it on the click, on the
reasoning that a menu of one is a wasted click. That was wrong, and the
owner's report says why: *"I want to know what it is first."*

The saved click cost the player the only chance to read what they were
about to do — and a card's ONE legal play is often not the one they had in
mind. A mode they cannot afford is not offered, so the mode that IS
offered may be the other one; a targeted card with one legal target aims
somewhere they have not checked; a granted action belongs to a minion they
did not pick. In every one of those cases the shortcut committed them to
something they had not seen.

So the menu opens for one play as readily as for five, in the hand and on
the table both. The badge already says how many, and "1" is a useful
answer rather than a reason to skip the step.

The cost is one extra click on the commonest case, which is the right
trade: this is a game where a misplayed card cannot be taken back, and the
menu is where `describePlay` spells the play out in English.
